Two Faces, One House

The idea behind this line is that a house has two audiences and they want different things. From the road it should be composed, quiet and in keeping with its neighbours. From the water or the view side it can open up completely.

That split is what lets these designs sit comfortably on a road frontage without giving up the drama where it counts. The roadside elevation carries the entry, the garage and the service rooms behind a restrained face; the opposite elevation carries the glass.

Timeless Rather Than Current

A design that is emphatically of its moment reads as dated within a decade, and a house is a thirty-year decision. The elevations in this line lean on proportion, roof form and material rather than on whatever detail is current, which is what the word timeless is doing in the description.

It also makes them easier to adapt. A plan whose character comes from proportion survives being lengthened, flipped or re-clad; one whose character comes from a signature detail usually does not.

Fitting the Line to a Lot

These suit a property with a clear front and back — a road on one side, something worth facing on the other. Where the ground falls away instead, the Country Retreats line is drawn around walkout basements; where the view dominates everything, the Panoramics are built around it.

Choosing between the lines is a question about the land far more than a question about taste, and it is the first conversation rather than the last.

Garages, and Where They Sit

On a plan with a composed road elevation, the garage is the element most likely to spoil it, because it is large and it faces the street. These designs generally set it back, turn it, or absorb it into the massing so it reads as part of the house rather than the first thing seen.

The practical consequence is worth knowing: a turned or detached garage usually costs more in foundation and roof than one tucked straight into the front elevation. It is one of the clearest examples of a design decision that is really a budget decision, and it is made early.

Adapting Without Losing the Character

Most changes clients ask for — lengthening the plan, adding a wing, growing the primary suite, vaulting the great room — sit comfortably within this line, because the character comes from proportion and roof form rather than from a single feature. What does need care is the roadside elevation, where adding width or height without adjusting the roof can tip a composed face into a sprawling one.

Roofline and Materials

The character of these elevations comes from roof form more than from ornament: pitch, how the gables are arranged, and where the roof breaks to mark an entry or a wing. It is why they adapt without losing themselves — lengthening a plan changes the proportion but not the language.

Material choice follows the same logic. These designs are built in a range of claddings and the composition still reads, because the composition is in the massing rather than in the finish.

The Entry Sequence

With a composed road elevation the entry has to be found easily, which is a design problem rather than a signage one. These plans generally mark it with a roof break, a projecting element or a covered porch, so the way in is obvious from the driveway without the door having to shout.

Inside, the sequence matters as much: arriving into a space that opens toward the view side is what connects the quiet front of the house to the open back, and it is the move that makes the two-faced idea work rather than feel like two different buildings.

Entertaining Flow

Where a house is organised around a view side, that is also where people gather, so the kitchen, dining and living spaces on these plans open to each other and to the glazing rather than being separated.

What is usually kept separate is the service side: pantry, laundry, mudroom and garage access on the road elevation, out of the sightline. The result is a main floor where the parts people see are open and the parts that get untidy are not.

Scaling the Plan Up or Down

These designs take length well. Adding a bay to the main floor, extending a wing, or stretching the plan along its axis keeps the elevation reading correctly because the proportion of roof to wall is maintained.

What they take less well is height added without reworking the roof. Raising a wall plate or adding a storey without adjusting the pitch flattens the composition, which is the one change here that genuinely needs redrawing rather than resizing.

Where These Sit Best

A lot with a clear public side and a private side, enough frontage to see the elevation from the approach, and a view or a lake behind. On a tight shoreline lot where the building is close to both the road and the water, the two-elevation idea has less room to work and a different line usually suits better.

Frequently Asked Questions

What defines the Country Classics line?

A stylish, distinctive roadside elevation paired with a dramatic, contemporary treatment on the lake or view side. The two faces of the house do different jobs: one meets the road quietly, the other opens up completely.

How many plans are in this line?

Seventeen. Each one has been built for a family in Central Ontario, so the layouts have been through real sites and real winters rather than existing only on paper.

Can these plans be customized?

Yes. Every plan in the collection is a starting point rather than a catalogue order — walls move, wings get added, and the whole plan can be flipped to face a different view.

Are these designs suited to a sloping lot?

Some are, and others adapt. Where the ground falls away, the Country Retreats line is drawn around walkout basements specifically, and any Country Classic can be adapted during design.

Are they built as cottages or as year-round homes?

Both. The difference is in the envelope, the heating and the servicing rather than the layout, and it is settled at design stage.

How do I choose between the four lines?

By the lot more than by taste. Country Classics suit a lot with a clear road side and view side, Country Retreats suit narrow or sloping ground, Panoramics are built around a spectacular view, and Post & Beam is a detailing choice that can go on any of them.
