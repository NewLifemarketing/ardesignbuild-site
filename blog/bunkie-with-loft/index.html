Bunkie With Loft

The Roof Decides Whether Anyone Can Sleep Up There

A bunkie with loft space puts a second level of beds on the same small footprint, and in Ontario the Building Code, not the floor plan, decides how tall the building has to be before that loft works.

That is the part most people meet late. The picture in their head is a cozy cabin with a ladder and a mattress tucked under the roof. The Code's picture is a floor, with a set height above it and below it, a guard along its edge and a proper way up.

I work with our clients from the first sketch through to municipal approvals, and a bunkie is one of the projects where those two pictures need to meet early. In Muskoka especially, a bunkie is how a cottage makes room for the grandkids all summer, and the loft is usually the reason the plan works. So it is worth knowing what the loft will ask of the building before anyone settles on a footprint.

What the Code calls a loft

The Ontario Building Code does not use the word loft. It uses mezzanine, and defines it as an intermediate floor assembly between the floor and ceiling of any room or storey, including an interior balcony. The sleeping platform in a bunkie with loft beds fits that description exactly.

That matters because a mezzanine has rules of its own, and they are written in metres, not in feelings about coziness. Three of them shape almost every design: the height above and below, how much of the room the loft may cover before it becomes a second storey, and how people get up and stay up there safely.

How tall a bunkie with loft beds has to be

Article 9.5.3.2 of the Code is one sentence long. The ceiling height above and below a mezzanine floor, in all occupancies, has to be at least 2.1 m.

Both sides. Not just the room underneath.

Stack it up and the numbers get real quickly. Take 2.1 m for the room below, add roughly 200 mm for the loft floor itself (a 2x8 joist is 38 x 184 mm before the subfloor goes on), then another 2.1 m above. That is about 4.4 m, a little over 14 feet 5 inches, from the main floor to the ceiling over the loft. For comparison, a typical single-storey bunkie with an 8-foot wall has its ceiling at 2.4 m.

Under a gable roof, that height only exists near the ridge. The loft floor runs out to where the slope comes down to meet it, and only the middle strip has the full 2.1 m. This is why a steep roof pitch matters so much on a small building, and why the loft and the roof have to be drawn together rather than one after the other. How your building department measures height under a sloped ceiling is worth asking them directly, with your drawings in front of you.

The roof is also where the insulation goes once the loft is heated. With the drywall fastened to the rafters there is no attic over a loft, so the rafter depth has to hold the insulation and the air space above it, the same problem we walk through for any vaulted ceiling.

When a bunkie with loft space becomes two storeys

Here is the rule that changes the whole conversation. Under Article 9.10.4.1, the space above a mezzanine can be left out of the building's height only if the mezzanine stays under one of two limits. Go past both and the loft is a storey, and your one-storey bunkie is now a two-storey building, with everything that brings at the township office.

The 10% route

The first limit is 10% of the floor area. In a 10 by 16 foot bunkie, 160 square feet in all, that is 16 square feet. A shelf for duffel bags, not a bed.

The 40% route for a bunkie with loft

The second limit is the useful one. A mezzanine can cover up to 40% of the open area of the room it sits in, provided the space above it stays open: no partitions or walls more than 1,070 mm above the loft floor. One enclosed space is allowed up there, as long as it is no bigger than 10% of the room's open area and does not block the view between the loft and the room below.

In that same 160 square foot bunkie, 40% is 64 square feet, an 8 by 8 foot platform. Room for a double mattress and a little floor beside it. What it cannot be is a walled-in bedroom with a door; the moment the loft is closed off, it stops being open area and the 40% route no longer applies.

Stacking a second loft above the first does not get around this. Article 9.10.4.2 counts every mezzanine level after the first as a storey.

Why the storey count matters in Muskoka

The Building Code asks whether the loft is safe. Your township's zoning asks whether the building is allowed at all, and how big and how tall it may be.

In cottage country, a sleeping cabin is usually a category of its own in the zoning by-law. The Township of Muskoka Lakes' Official Plan, for example, allows one dwelling and one sleeping cabin per residential waterfront property, where the lot dimensions are appropriate (Section B, policy 10.3). The details, floor area, height and setbacks from the water and the lot lines, sit in each municipality's by-law and they differ from one township to the next.

Which is why the 40% line is more than a technicality. A one-storey cap on a sleeping cabin, or a height limit written in metres, is far easier to meet with a loft the Code does not count as a storey than with a second floor that it does. Ask the planning department how its by-law treats a loft before the design is fixed, not after.

Stairs, guards and a smoke alarm

Plenty of bunkie kits show a ladder up to the loft. Part 9 of the Code sets out how stairs are built, and a loft people sleep in is not one of the spaces its stair table treats as unoccupied. Plan for a stair from the start, and treat a ladder as something to raise with your building department rather than assume.

The stair table itself raises a question worth asking. Its private-stair limits, a maximum rise of 200 mm and a minimum run of 255 mm, are written for stairs inside a dwelling unit. Every other stair falls under the public limits, 180 mm and 280 mm, which take more floor. A bunkie with no kitchen is not usually a dwelling unit, so find out which row your building department will hold you to before the stair is drawn. On a tight footprint, a spiral stair is the other option: Article 9.8.4.7 allows one with a 660 mm clear width and risers up to 240 mm, as the only way up where it serves no more than three people.

Then the edge. Any mezzanine with a drop of more than 600 mm needs a guard along it (Article 9.8.8.1). The general height is 1,070 mm; the lower 900 mm figure in Article 9.8.8.3 is for guards within dwelling units, so the same question about how your bunkie is classified applies here too. The guard cannot have openings a 100 mm sphere could pass through, and nothing on it between 140 mm and 900 mm off the floor can make it easy to climb. In a building full of grandkids, that last rule earns its place.

Finally, the Code requires a smoke alarm in each sleeping room that is not within a dwelling unit (Article 9.10.19.1), with a visual signal as well as the sound. Smoke rises, and in a bunkie with loft beds the people asleep at the top are the ones who need the earliest warning.

A permit, whatever the size

People sometimes plan a bunkie around the 15 m² shed exemption. It does not stretch that far. The exemption in the Code (Division C, Article 1.3.1.1) covers a shed of up to 15 m² that is one storey, freestanding, used only for storage and has no plumbing. A bed is not storage, so a building people sleep in needs a building permit at any size. We set out the shed rule in more detail in our post on post and beam sheds.

That is no bad thing for a bunkie with loft beds. A permit means the stair, the guard and the floor that holds the loft are checked against the Code before anyone sleeps up there, and the township has signed off on the building's height and location before it goes up.

Planning one with us

We design and build bunkies and sleeping cabins for overflow cottage guests, matched to the main building, properly permitted and built to last Central Ontario winters. That includes full permit handling and the zoning work for secondary structures, which is where the loft question gets answered.

If you are picturing a bunkie with loft beds, bring three things to the first conversation: how many people need to sleep in it, roughly where on the lot it will sit, and whether it will be used in winter. Those decide the footprint, the roof pitch and the insulation, and together they decide whether the loft fits. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you build a bunkie with loft beds in Ontario?

Yes, with a building permit and a loft designed to the Building Code from the start. The Code treats the loft as a mezzanine, so it needs at least 2.1 m of ceiling height above and below it, a guard along any edge with a drop of more than 600 mm, and a stair your building department accepts. Zoning decides separately whether a sleeping cabin is allowed on your lot and how tall it can be.

How tall does a bunkie need to be for a loft?

Roughly 4.4 m, or about 14 feet 5 inches, from the main floor to the ceiling over the loft. That is 2.1 m below the loft, about 200 mm for the loft floor, and 2.1 m above it, as Article 9.5.3.2 of the Code requires. Under a gable roof only the middle of the loft reaches that height, so a steeper pitch gives you more usable floor.

Does a loft count as a second storey?

Not if it stays within one of the Code's limits. Under Article 9.10.4.1, the loft can cover up to 10% of the floor area, or up to 40% of the open area of the room if the space above it stays open, with no walls more than 1,070 mm above the loft floor. Past those limits, or with a second loft level, it counts as a storey.

How big can a bunkie loft be?

Under the 40% route, up to 40% of the open area of the room it sits in. In a 10 by 16 foot bunkie, that is 64 square feet, about an 8 by 8 foot platform: enough for a double mattress with a little floor beside it.

Do I need a permit for a bunkie in Muskoka?

Yes. The Code's permit exemption for small buildings covers sheds of up to 15 m² used only for storage, so a building people sleep in needs a building permit at any size. Your township's zoning by-law also sets where a sleeping cabin can go, how big it can be and how tall, so check with its planning department early.

Can I use a ladder to reach a bunkie loft?

Do not count on it. Part 9 of the Code sets out how stairs are built, and a loft used for sleeping is not one of the spaces its stair table treats as unoccupied. Plan for a stair, or a spiral stair where space is tight, and raise anything else with your building department before the design is final.
