Cottage Plans With Wrap Around Porches

A Porch That Turns the Corner

Cottage plans with wrap around porches look simple on a rendering: a roof on posts, carried around a corner of the house.

Built, that roof is a piece of structure that has to hold a Georgian Bay winter, stand on footings below the frost, and sit in front of windows you will look through every day of the year. None of that shows on a plan catalogue page, and all of it decides whether the porch becomes the room you live on from May to October or a dark, snow-loaded edge you walk past.

Turning a plan into something buildable on a particular lot is the part of the work I do with clients, so this is the conversation I would have with you about a porch before anything is drawn: which sides it should wrap, what it does to the rooms behind it, and what the code asks of it here.

What cottage plans with wrap around porches are really drawing

A porch is an outdoor floor with a roof over it. A wrap-around porch is one that does not stop at the end of a wall: it turns the corner and keeps going down a second side, sometimes a third. That one turn is what makes it different from a front porch with a deck at the back. The porch becomes a way around the house as well as a place to sit in it.

On a drawing, the porch is usually a band of hatching with a line of posts. In the building it is three things at once. It is a roof, tied into the house's own roof, that has to shed water and carry snow. It is a row of posts, and every post needs a footing. And it is a floor, often raised above the ground, with a railing that the code has opinions about.

Each of those is a decision. Cottage plans with wrap around porches that look alike from the road can differ a great deal in how well they answer them.

Two plans from our catalogue that already wrap the corner

Our home plans catalogue holds 82 plans across four lines, and two of them show what a porch that turns the corner does to the rest of a plan particularly well. Neither is offered as the answer for your lot. Like every plan we hold, each one is a starting point.

The Edwardian: a porch that becomes the side entrance

The Edwardian is a three-bedroom, two-and-a-half-bath plan with 1,766 sq ft of living area on a 35 by 30 foot footprint. Its floor plan shows the covered porch running across the front past the entry, turning the corner, and running the full length of the side past the kitchen and laundry until it meets the deck off the dining room.

That is a porch doing real work. The kitchen side of the house gets a sheltered way in and out, and the front door and the back deck are joined by a covered walk. On a compact two-storey footprint, the porch is most of what makes the house feel generous at ground level.

The Acadian: a porch on three sides

The Acadian goes further. It is a three-bedroom, two-and-a-half-bath plan with 1,948 sq ft of living area on a 42 by 39 foot footprint, the primary bedroom on the main floor and a great room open to the floor above. Its floor plan carries the covered porch along the front, down one side and across the back, so the great room and dining room open onto it on more than one side.

This is the shape most people picture when they ask about a wrap-around porch. It is also the shape that asks the most of the questions below, because the more of the house a porch roof covers, the more it decides about light, snow and structure.

Which sides the porch should wrap

A porch that wraps every side looks complete on a rendering and is rarely the best use of the money. The useful question is which sides you will actually use.

  • The view side. On a waterfront lot this is where people sit, and it is where a covered porch earns its keep. Our design and plan customization work starts from the same place: the main rooms face the view, and the plan is flipped or rotated until they do.
  • The arrival side. A porch at the front marks the way in. In our Country Classics line the entry is generally marked with a roof break, a projecting element or a covered porch, so it can be found from the driveway.
  • The sun. A porch on the west catches the evening; one on the south shades the rooms behind it at midday. A porch on the north mostly makes a dark room darker.
  • The working side. A covered route from the car to the kitchen, as on The Edwardian, is worth a great deal at a cottage in October.

Often the right answer is a porch that wraps one corner, from the arrival side round to the view side, with an open deck or no porch at all on the remaining walls. It reads as a wrap-around from the approach and costs a fraction of one that circles the house.

The porch roof and the rooms behind it

A covered porch is a large overhang, and an overhang changes the room behind it. Sized and placed well, it does what our Panoramics plans use overhangs for: it shades high summer sun and lets the low winter sun through. A covered porch also extends the season at both ends, which is much of the point of one at a cottage.

Placed carelessly, a deep porch roof in front of the main living room takes daylight the room needed, and it does it all year. A room that sits behind a porch on two sides can feel dim even in July. That is why the windows behind a wrap-around porch deserve as much attention as the porch: taller glass, a clerestory above the porch roof, or simply leaving the porch off the one wall where the room most needs light. The same thinking runs through choosing windows for the walls you sit beside all winter.

Snow on cottage plans with wrap around porches near Georgian Bay

The porch roof is a roof, and the code sizes it like one. Under Ontario's Building Code the snow a roof must carry starts from the ground snow load for the place it is built, published in Supplementary Standard SB-1, and the roof load is worked out from it. For a narrow roof, no more than 4.3 metres across, the code uses a smaller factor on that ground figure than it does for wider roofs. Most porch roofs are that narrow.

The ground figure is where Georgian Bay shows itself. SB-1 lists a ground snow load of 2.7 kPa for Midland and 2.8 kPa for Penetanguishene, against 2.5 kPa for Barrie, a short drive inland. A porch framed to a figure remembered from somewhere else is framed to the wrong number.

Two more things matter on a porch in particular. The floor of a covered porch can still collect drifting snow, and the code asks that decks and other outdoor platforms exposed to snow be designed for the roof snow load or 1.9 kPa, whichever is greater. And snow does not stay on the main roof. When it slides, it lands on whatever is below, which on a wrap-around design is the porch roof. Where the two roofs meet, at the valleys around each corner, is where the detailing has to be best. Our post on metal and shingle roofs on Georgian Bay covers how the choice of roofing changes how snow comes off.

Posts, footings and the frost

Every porch post carries a share of that roof, and every post stands on a footing. Ground that freezes expands, and a footing resting in it gets lifted in winter and dropped in spring. Under a porch roof that is tied into the house, that movement does not stay at the post. It shows up as a sagging beam, a cracked joint where the porch roof meets the main roof, or a door on the porch that sticks every March.

How deep a footing goes is set by the foundation rules in Part 9 of the code and by the soil: on clay, or soil that is not clearly identified, foundations that do not enclose heated space go below the depth of frost penetration. Our post on concrete footings covers how they are sized. A wrap-around porch has a lot of posts, so this is a real share of the cost, and the reason a long porch costs more than its decking suggests.

Railings, height and the view

A porch floor more than 600 mm above the ground needs a guard. For a house, an exterior guard can be 900 mm high where the porch floor is no more than 1.8 metres above the ground, and 1,070 mm where it is higher. Openings in it must stop a 100 mm sphere from passing through. On a sloping waterfront lot the porch can start at the ground on the road side and finish well above it on the lake side, so one porch can need both heights.

Glass is the usual answer when the view matters, and the code allows it if it is laminated or tempered safety glass. The cottage of ours that Our Homes magazine featured in the Tiny Beaches area of Georgian Bay used glass railings for exactly that reason, to keep the view of the bay unobstructed, with a partially covered back deck made for long summer meals.

Cottage plans with wrap around porches start on paper

A porch added after the house is framed means new footings, a roof cut into a finished roof, and flashing worked into siding that is already on. On a drawing it is a revision. That is the whole case for settling it during design.

What to check on cottage plans with wrap around porches

  • Which rooms open onto it, and whether those are the rooms you will use most.
  • What it covers, and whether the rooms behind the deepest part still get the light they need.
  • Where the roof meets the main roof, and how many corners and valleys that makes.
  • How high the floor sits, at every side, since that sets the railing.
  • Whether part of it should be more than a porch. A screened porch stays outdoors. An all-season room is glazed, insulated and heated, which makes it part of the building envelope, so on our plans that change is decided during design because it changes the foundation and the envelope rather than just the finish.

On cost, the porch sits squarely in the things that move a budget. Footprint, roof complexity and the number of corners move it far more than finishes do, and a porch that wraps a house adds to all three. That is not a reason to leave one off. It is a reason to put it where it will be used. If you are reading plans now, our post on what actually gets changed on a stock plan is the next thing worth reading.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a wrap-around porch be added to any of your cottage plans?

Every plan in our catalogue is a starting point. Walls move, wings get added and plans are flipped to face the view, and a porch is a change of the same kind when it is made on the drawing. Whether it suits a given plan depends on which rooms it would cover and how the roof would meet the main roof, and that is worked out with you during design.

Are cottage plans with wrap around porches more expensive to build?

Yes, by more than the decking suggests. A porch adds roof, corners and footings, and footprint, roof complexity and the number of corners are among the things that move a budget most. Wrapping one corner, from the arrival side round to the view, usually gives most of the effect for a fraction of a porch that circles the house.

Does a covered porch make the inside of the house darker?

It can. A porch roof is a deep overhang, and the room directly behind it gets less daylight all year. Placed on the south it shades high summer sun and still lets low winter sun in; placed in front of the main living room on two sides, it can leave that room dim. Taller windows or a clerestory above the porch roof help.

Does a porch roof need to be built for snow?

Yes. Ontario's Building Code sizes it like any other roof, from the ground snow load published for the location in Supplementary Standard SB-1, which is 2.7 kPa in Midland and 2.8 kPa in Penetanguishene. A porch floor exposed to drifting snow is designed for the roof snow load or 1.9 kPa, whichever is greater.

Should a wrap-around porch be screened or turned into a sunroom?

A screened porch is still an outdoor room, built like a porch. A sunroom that is glazed, insulated and heated becomes part of the house's envelope, with the foundation and insulation that implies. Decide which you want before the drawings are finished, because changing a porch into a heated room later means rebuilding what is under it.
