Is a Metal Roof Better Than a Shingle Roof?

Two Good Roofs, and the Four Questions That Separate Them

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof? Not everywhere, and what decides it on a Georgian Bay build is the slope you are putting it on, the snow it has to carry, how many valleys the roof has, and how long you plan to own the building.

Roofing is one of the selections clients make with us in the Design Centre, alongside siding, stone, windows and trim. It is also the selection people arrive with the strongest opinion about and the least information behind it, usually something a neighbour said about noise, or about a roof that lasts forever.

So here is the version I give at the table. Several things genuinely separate these two materials on a Central Ontario build, and only one of them is about looks.

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof on a shallow pitch? The Code answers that one

Before taste gets a say, Table 9.26.3.1 of the Ontario Building Code sets a minimum slope for every roof covering, and the two materials do not get the same number.

Asphalt shingles in a normal application need at least 1 in 3, which is 4 in 12. Profiled metal roofing and sheet metal shingles need 1 in 4, which is 3 in 12. There is no maximum for either, so a steep roof is open to both. For comparison, the same table puts cedar shakes at 1 in 3, wood shingles at 1 in 4, and slate and clay tile at 1 in 2.

That gap matters on the shallow parts of a building. A porch roof, a shed dormer, a low-pitched wing off the back of a house: at 3 in 12 the metal is allowed and the shingles are not, unless they go on as a low-slope application under Subsection 9.26.8, which has its own coverage and fastening rules. Below 3 in 12, Sentence 9.26.3.1.(3) lets profiled metal systems that are specifically designed for low slopes go lower still, installed the way the manufacturer writes it down.

This is why the two materials so often end up on the same house. The main roof carries shingles and the low porch roof carries metal, and that is not a compromise. It is each material on the slope it was made for.

What a Georgian Bay roof is actually holding up

The Code publishes a ground snow load for every town in Ontario, and the numbers along Georgian Bay are among the higher ones in the south. Supplementary Standard SB-1 gives Midland 2.7 kPa, Penetanguishene and Parry Sound 2.8, and Barrie 2.5. Each of those carries a separate 0.4 kPa rain load on top, which is the Code's way of accounting for the February afternoon when it rains onto a roof already full of snow.

Put 2.7 kPa over a 10 by 10 metre footprint and you are holding roughly 2,750 kilograms. That load is carried by the trusses or rafters underneath, not by the roofing, and it is the same whichever material is on top. A metal roof is lighter than asphalt, which is occasionally useful on a re-roof over an existing structure, but on a new build the frame is engineered for the snow either way.

The same table decides something else. Eave protection under Article 9.26.5.1 is required on shingle, shake and tile roofs, running from the edge of the roof at least 900 mm up the slope and to a line not less than 300 mm inside the inner face of the exterior wall. One of the exemptions is for regions with 3,500 or fewer degree-days below 18 degrees. Midland and Penetanguishene sit at 4,200, Barrie at 4,380, Parry Sound at 4,640. Nowhere around this bay gets out of it.

Two of the other exemptions are worth knowing because they come up on real drawings. Eave protection is not required over unheated garages, carports and porches, and it is not required where the roof overhang is more than 900 mm measured along the slope from the edge of the roof to the inner face of the wall. A deep overhang is doing the same job the membrane does, by keeping the heated wall well back from the cold edge.

Snow that slides, and where it lands

A metal roof sheds snow, and that is sold as an advantage. It is, right up until you think about what is underneath the eave.

Shingles hold snow in place through a thaw and let it go in pieces. A smooth metal panel can release a whole slope at once, usually on the first warm afternoon after a cold spell, and a winter's worth of snow coming off a two-storey roof is not a small event. If the entry, the walkway, the deck, the propane tank or where people park sits under that slope, the roof needs snow guards, and those are a design decision rather than an afterthought.

On a cottage nobody visits between December and April, that same shedding is a genuine benefit: less load sitting on the roof, less melt and refreeze at the eave, fewer things to go wrong while the building is empty. On a house people walk in and out of every day, the snow has to be told where to go.

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof over forty years of ownership?

This is where the gap is real and worth paying attention to. InterNACHI's life expectancy chart, which home inspectors across North America work from, puts three-tab asphalt shingles at about 20 years and architectural asphalt at about 30. Metal is listed at 40 to 80, and copper at 70 or more.

Set that against how long you expect to own the building. A metal roof that outlives two shingle roofs is excellent value across 40 years and a premium you may never recover across 12. Neither answer is wrong. They are answers to different questions, and the question is yours rather than the roofer's.

Worth saying plainly: those are ranges, not promises, and nobody re-roofs on a schedule. A roof fails at its details long before the field of it wears out, which is why the sections below spend more time on valleys, flashings and fasteners than on the material itself.

Metal is not one product, and the Code says so

The span on that metal figure is wide for a reason. It covers everything from a thin profiled panel screwed through the face to a standing-seam copper roof with concealed clips, and those two things have almost nothing in common except the word.

The Code is unusually blunt about this. Asphalt shingles have to conform to CSA A123.5, a product standard with test requirements behind it. Sheet metal roofing has no product standard listed in Table 9.26.2.1.-B at all. What Article 9.26.13.1 sets instead is a minimum thickness: 0.33 mm for galvanized steel, 0.46 mm for copper and zinc, 0.48 mm for aluminum. Thickness is a floor, not a grade.

So two metal quotes can be much further apart in quality than two shingle quotes, and the difference will not be visible on the roof the week it goes on. Ask for the gauge, the coating system and whether the fasteners are exposed or concealed.

That last one matters most over time. An exposed-fastener panel is held down by screws driven through the face of the metal, each with a rubber washer, and those washers are the part that ages. A standing-seam panel is held by clips hidden in the seam, so the weather never reaches the fastener. It is worth noticing that for asphalt shingles the Code simply requires it: Article 9.26.7.4 says shingles are to be fastened so that no nails or staples are exposed.

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof on a complicated roof? Usually not

The price difference between the two is not a fixed multiple, and the roof in the photograph below is the reason.

Shingles are small units. They go around a dormer, into a valley and up to a chimney with cuts a roofer makes on the spot. Metal arrives as long panels sized for a particular run, and every hip, valley, dormer and skylight is a cut, a flashing and a detail somebody has to get right.

A valley is the clearest example. Article 9.26.4.3 requires every valley on a shingled roof to be flashed, over continuous sheathing, and it is specific about how: closed valley flashing of sheet metal, self-sealing composite membrane or roll roofing at least 600 mm wide, with nails kept back at least 75 mm from the edge of the flashing and 124 mm from the bottom of the valley centre line. It also rules out closed valleys with rigid shingles on slopes shallower than 1 in 1.2. Each of those valleys is a piece of hand work, and on a metal roof there is a panel end to close on both sides of it as well.

Flashing itself has a minimum thickness, under Article 9.26.4.2: 0.33 mm galvanized steel or copper, 0.35 mm zinc, 0.48 mm aluminum, 1.73 mm sheet lead. On a simple gable with two long uninterrupted slopes, metal is at its most competitive and there is very little of this work. On a roof like this one, the extra labour and flashing rise much faster than the material does.

If you want a straight comparison, ask for both prices on your drawings rather than on a square-foot rule of thumb, because the rule of thumb is exactly what the complexity breaks. The same principle runs through what drives the cost of a custom build generally: shape costs more than size, and it costs most where the material is least forgiving of being cut.

The noise question

Almost everyone asks about rain on a metal roof, and the mental image behind it is a barn or a shed: panels fastened to purlins with nothing but air underneath.

A house is not built that way. The panels sit on solid decking, over underlayment, above an insulated and often vaulted ceiling, and that assembly absorbs most of what the barn roof broadcasts. The Code even distinguishes the two cases. Article 9.26.13.2 only asks for panels designed to carry the roof live loads where the metal is not supported by decking but spans between spaced supports.

Rain is audible on a metal roof. It is nothing like the shed, and on an open vaulted ceiling with less assembly between you and the sky, it is more audible than in a bedroom under an attic. Worth a question about your own ceiling, not worth ruling the material out.

Neither one fixes an ice dam

Ice dams get blamed on roofing, and people switch material hoping to solve them. It rarely works, because the cause is underneath.

An ice dam forms when heat escaping into the roof assembly melts snow high on the slope, the water runs down to the cold overhang past the heated walls, and it refreezes there. The fix is air sealing, insulation and ventilation, which is why how you insulate a vaulted ceiling matters more to a dry roof than the material on top of it.

Metal can make a dam less likely by shedding snow before it has time to melt and refreeze, and it is less damaged than shingles when one forms. But a warm roof with a cold eave will still make ice, and on a metal roof the water backing up behind it finds the same seams and fasteners. Build the assembly right and the covering is a choice. Build it wrong and the covering is a scapegoat.

What to make your quote specify

Most of the disappointment I see comes from a quote that named a material and nothing else. Whichever way you go, ask that the price name these.

For a metal roof: the gauge and the coating system, whether the fasteners are exposed or concealed, what is going under the panels, how valleys and sidewall flashings are detailed, and whether snow guards are included and where. For shingles: the shingle line and its warranty, the underlay, and how far up the slope the eave protection runs.

The Code gives you the floor on that last item and it is worth checking against the quote. Eave protection goes beneath the starter strip and may be two plies of No. 15 asphalt-saturated felt lapped 480 mm, Type M or S roll roofing, coated base sheets, or a self-adhering membrane. Underlay above it is installed parallel to the eaves with laps of at least 50 mm and has to overlap the eave protection by at least 100 mm. Numbers like these are easy to confirm and hard to argue with afterwards.

What we ask before you choose

Our build packages note that the envelope, the framing, the roof and the mechanical systems are built to the same standard whichever package you choose, because they are the parts nobody can economically revisit later. The roof is firmly in that group. You will re-pick a backsplash one day; you will not casually re-roof a house.

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof? That is not really one question, it is five, and we work through them in this order. What is the pitch, and does it rule anything out. How complicated is the roof, and how many valleys does the material have to be cut into. What sits under the eaves in February. How long do you intend to own it. And does the material belong on this building and this shoreline, which is the part that is genuinely about taste and the part I can help you look at properly.

Bring the drawings to the Design Centre and we will price both, on your roof rather than on a general rule, and tell you plainly where the difference comes from.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a metal roof better than a shingle roof for a cottage left empty all winter?

For an unoccupied building it usually is. A metal roof sheds snow rather than holding it through every thaw, which means less load sitting on the roof and less melt and refreeze at the eave while nobody is there to notice. Fit snow guards anywhere the slope discharges over an entry, a deck or a propane tank.

Can I put metal on a low-pitched roof where shingles are not allowed?

Often yes. Table 9.26.3.1 of the Ontario Building Code sets 1 in 4, or 3 in 12, as the minimum slope for profiled metal roofing and sheet metal shingles, against 1 in 3, or 4 in 12, for asphalt shingles in a normal application. Below 3 in 12, Sentence 9.26.3.1.(3) allows profiled metal systems specifically designed for low slopes, installed to the manufacturer's written instructions.

Does a metal roof need ice and water protection at the eaves?

Article 9.26.5.1 requires eave protection on shingle, shake and tile roofs, so a standing-seam metal roof is not caught by that sentence, though sheet metal shingles are. That is a question of what the Code compels, not of what is wise. Along Georgian Bay the degree-day exemption is no help either way: it applies at 3,500 degree-days or fewer, and Midland and Penetanguishene are 4,200, Barrie 4,380 and Parry Sound 4,640. Ask your designer what is going under your roof at the eave, and why.

Will a metal roof save money on insurance or heating?

Treat both claims with caution. Insurance rates are set by the insurer, not the roofer, so get it in writing from your own broker before it counts toward the budget. Heating is decided by air sealing and insulation in the ceiling assembly, not by the material above the decking.

How much longer does a metal roof last?

InterNACHI's life expectancy chart puts three-tab asphalt shingles at around 20 years and architectural asphalt at 30, with metal at 40 to 80 and copper at 70 or more. The spread on metal is wide because the category runs from thin profiled panel to standing-seam copper, so ask about gauge, coating and fastener type rather than treating metal as one product.

Can you mix the two on one house?

Yes, and it is common here. Shingles on the main slopes with metal on porch roofs, dormer tops or a low rear wing is a normal detail, and on shallow pitches it is sometimes the only way those sections work at all.
