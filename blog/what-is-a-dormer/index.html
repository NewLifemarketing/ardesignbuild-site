What Is a Dormer

A Window That Brings Its Own Wall

What is a dormer? It is a small framed structure that stands up out of a sloping roof, with its own front wall, its own little roof and, almost always, a window.

That is the dictionary answer, and it is correct. What it leaves out is the reason anyone draws one. A dormer turns the space under a roof slope, where you can only stand up in the middle, into a room with a vertical window and somewhere to stand in front of it. It is a window that brings its own wall.

Turning ideas into buildable plans is the part of the work I do with clients, and a dormer is decided on those plans long before anyone cuts a rafter. So this is what I would tell you about them across the table: what one is, what it does for the room behind it, how to tell one from a gable, and what has to be settled before it is built.

What is a dormer, in plain terms

Picture a roof slope. Now cut a rectangle out of it and build a small box up through the hole, with a flat front wall facing out, two short side walls (builders call them cheeks), and a roof of its own on top. Put a window in the front wall. That box is the dormer.

Merriam-Webster defines it as a window set vertically in a structure projecting through a sloping roof, and also as the roofed structure that holds that window. Both meanings are in everyday use. When someone says "the dormer window," they usually mean the window. When a framer says "the dormer," they mean the whole box, rafters and all.

The important word in both is vertical. The window in a dormer stands up straight, like any other window in a wall. That is what separates it from a skylight, which lies in the plane of the roof.

What a dormer does for the room behind it

The most useful answer to what is a dormer comes from inside the room, not from the street. Under a roof slope, the ceiling comes down to meet the floor. A room up there has a strip of full height along the middle and a band at each side where you crouch, hit your head, or put storage. A dormer pushes part of that sloped ceiling up and out, and three things change.

  • Standing room. In front of the dormer window you get a patch of full ceiling height where there was none. Enough for a desk, a reading chair, a bathroom vanity, or the head of a bed.
  • Daylight and a view. A vertical window sees the horizon rather than the sky, and it is easy to shade and easy to open.
  • Air and a way out. An opening window in an upper room gives cross-ventilation in summer, and in a bedroom it can be the window the code requires for getting out in an emergency.

What a dormer does not do is add much floor area. The floor was already there. A dormer makes more of that floor usable, which on a storey-and-a-half house is often the difference between a real bedroom and a room only a child can stand up in.

The common shapes, and what each is good at

Ask what is a dormer and most people picture one shape, but dormers come in several, usually named after their roof. These are the ones you will see on most house plans.

Gable dormer

A small peaked roof over a single window, like the two on our build in the photo above. It is the most familiar shape and the easiest to make look right on a traditional house. It adds a modest amount of headroom: the space in front of one window.

Shed dormer

A single flat roof plane, pitched more shallowly than the main roof, running across a wide front wall. Because it can be long, it can carry several windows and adds the most usable floor of any shape. The trade-off is its low slope, which narrows the roofing choices. Our post on metal versus shingle roofing explains why shallow sections like this are often done in metal.

Hip and eyebrow dormers

A hip dormer has its roof sloping back on three sides, which reads quieter on a hip-roofed house. An eyebrow dormer has a curved roof that flows into the main roof with no cheeks at all. It is mostly about character, and it lets in light rather than adding headroom.

What is a dormer, and what is only a gable

The two are easy to mix up on a rendering, and the photo at the top of this post shows the difference well.

A gable is the triangular end of a roof. A cross gable is a second roof running the other way, so its triangle faces the street. Its wall comes up from the wall below; the tall cedar-shake section in the middle of that photo is one. A dormer starts partway up the roof. There is roof below it, roof on each side of it, and the dormer sits on top like a box set on a slope. The two navy ones on either side are dormers.

The difference matters for two reasons. A cross gable usually means the wall and the roof framing below are carrying it in the ordinary way. A dormer means rafters have been cut and a hole framed into the roof, with the load carried around it. And a dormer leaves roof on three sides of it to be flashed and kept dry, which a gable does not.

A skylight is different again. It is a window in the roof slope, lying at the roof's angle. It brings in more light for its size than a dormer window, but it adds no headroom and you cannot see out of it standing up.

What is a dormer doing on a real plan? The Brookside

The easiest way to understand dormers is to match them to the rooms they serve. The Brookside, from the Country Classics line in our home plans catalogue, makes that easy. It is a 3-bedroom, 3-bathroom plan with 2,850 sq ft of living area, and its rendering shows three gable dormers on the front roof, above a covered porch that runs the width of the house.

Now read the upper-floor plan on the same page. Bedroom #2 and Bedroom #3 sit at the two front corners, and each has a small projection in its front wall: that is where the outer dormers land, giving each bedroom a window and a spot of full height. Between them, at the front, the plan is marked "open to entry below." The middle dormer, with the arched window, is lighting the entry hall two floors down. The master bedroom is on the main floor, and the great room is open to above.

That is a dormer doing three jobs on one roof: two bedrooms made livable and an entry made bright. Like every plan in the catalogue, The Brookside is a starting point. If you would rather have a shed dormer across the back for a bigger third bedroom, that is the kind of change that belongs on the drawings.

Where a dormer meets the roof

Every dormer interrupts a roof, and a roof is only as dry as its worst joint. Each side of a dormer meets the main roof along a line that has to be flashed, and where the dormer's own roof meets the main slope it forms valleys. Ontario's Building Code requires every valley on a shingled roof to be flashed, and a closed valley's flashing has to be at least 600 mm wide.

Snow is the other half. A dormer is a wall standing on a roof, and snow sliding down from above builds up against its back. The roof around it is designed for the ground snow load published for your municipality in the province's Supplementary Standard SB-1, and the framing around the hole in the roof carries what the cut rafters used to. On a shingled roof, eave protection runs at least 900 mm up the slope from the edge, which matters on a dormer roof too: it is a small roof with its own eave, right where ice forms.

None of this is a reason to avoid dormers. It is a reason to keep the number sensible and the shapes simple. Every extra dormer adds two cheeks, a front wall, a small roof and its flashing.

Insulating the cheeks and the roof around them

A dormer adds a lot of outside surface for the floor it serves: a front wall, two side walls and a roof, each of which has to be insulated and air-sealed like any other wall or roof of the house. The cheeks are the awkward part. They are small, they are triangular, and the cold side of each one faces open air.

The roof around the dormer is harder still. Where a ceiling follows the rafters, the code asks for a vented space between the insulation and the roof sheathing, at least 63 mm deep, with vents low and high. A dormer cuts across those rafter bays, and a bay that is blocked at the top by a dormer has no way to vent. Our post on insulating a vaulted ceiling goes through how those bays are handled. On a drawing, it is a detail. On a finished roof, a missed one is a damp patch in the ceiling every spring.

A bedroom in a dormer needs a way out

When a dormer serves a bedroom, its window often has a second job. Ontario's Building Code requires every floor level with a bedroom to have a window or door that opens from the inside without tools, and a window doing that has to give an unobstructed opening of at least 0.35 m², with no side narrower than 380 mm, and stay open on its own.

Dormer windows are often small, because the dormer itself is small. A window that looks generous from the street can come up short once the frame and the sash are subtracted. If the dormer is meant to be the bedroom's way out, the window has to be chosen for that from the start, and the dormer sized to fit it.

Decide a dormer on paper

Adding a dormer to a finished house means opening a roof that is already shingled, cutting rafters that are already carrying load, and working the new walls and flashing into a building that is already closed in. Ontario's Building Code Act counts any material alteration of a building as construction, and no construction can happen without a permit from the chief building official. So a dormer added later needs drawings, a permit and inspections, on top of the work itself.

On a drawing, the same dormer is a revision. Footprint, roof complexity, the number of corners, ceiling heights and how much glass a building carries move a budget far more than finishes do, and a dormer touches three of those. That is not an argument against them. It is an argument for putting each one where it earns its place.

What is a dormer going to change on your drawings?

  • Which room it serves, and whether that room needs headroom, light, a way out, or all three.
  • Its shape, a single gable dormer for a window seat or a shed dormer for a full-height wall of room.
  • What is above and beside it, since every valley it creates has to be flashed and every rafter bay it blocks has to be dealt with.
  • The window, sized for egress if it is a bedroom, and for the view either way.
  • How many. Two well-placed dormers usually do more than four decorative ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a dormer on a house?

A dormer is a small framed structure that projects up out of a sloping roof, with its own front wall, side walls and roof, and usually a window in the front. It gives the room under the roof a vertical window, daylight and a patch of full ceiling height where the slope would otherwise come down.

What is the purpose of a dormer?

To make the space under a roof slope usable. A dormer adds standing room in front of its window, brings in light and air, and in a bedroom can provide the opening window the Building Code requires for getting out in an emergency. It adds little floor area; it makes more of the existing floor livable.

What is the difference between a dormer and a gable?

A gable is the triangular end of a roof, and its wall comes up from the wall below. A dormer starts partway up a roof slope, with roof below it and on both sides, and sits on the slope like a box. Structurally, a dormer means a hole framed into the roof and rafters cut around it, which a gable does not.

Do you need a permit to add a dormer in Ontario?

Yes. Ontario's Building Code Act treats a material alteration of a building as construction, and construction needs a permit from your municipality's chief building official. Adding a dormer cuts into the roof structure, so it needs drawings, a permit and inspections.

Is a dormer better than a skylight?

They do different jobs. A skylight lets in more light for its size and costs less, but it adds no headroom and you cannot see out of it standing up. A dormer gives a vertical window you can open and look through, plus usable height in front of it. In an upper bedroom, a dormer is usually the better answer.
