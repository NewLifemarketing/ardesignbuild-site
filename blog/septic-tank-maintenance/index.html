Septic Tank Maintenance

A Short List, Kept Every Year

Septic tank maintenance is a short list, and on most Muskoka properties the trouble comes from the one item nobody remembered, not from the tank itself.

The list is short enough to fit on a card by the back door. Know where the system is. Have the tank opened and checked every few years. Clean the filter once a year. Keep the wrong things out of the drains and keep water and weight off the bed. Write down what was done. Most of the systems that end up in trouble had an owner who did four of those and forgot the fifth.

On our builds, the septic system is part of site preparation, which I oversee, from design and installation through to the permit inspections. So I see these systems at the start of their lives, and I hear about them later when something has gone wrong. Here is the routine I would give anyone with a tank and a bed, whether it serves a house in town or a cottage that sits empty half the year.

What septic tank maintenance actually asks of you

A conventional system has two working parts. The tank holds the household's wastewater long enough for the heavy solids to settle to the bottom as sludge and the grease to float to the top as scum. The liquid in between flows out through an effluent filter to the leaching bed, where the soil does the rest of the treatment.

Septic tank maintenance is about protecting the bed by keeping the tank doing its job. A tank that is too full of sludge, or a filter that is clogged, sends solids where they were never meant to go. The bed is the expensive part. Once its soil is clogged, the repair can mean digging it out.

The province's SepticSmart guide, published through Ontario.ca, boils the work down to a Do list and a Don't list. Everything below comes from that guide, the Ontario Building Code, or the townships that inspect these systems, with notes on what changes in cottage country. If you want the longer view, including how long a system lasts and the warning signs of one that is failing, our post on how long a septic system lasts covers that ground.

Know where it is before you need it

It sounds too obvious to say, but plenty of owners could not point to their tank lids. The first item on SepticSmart's Do list is to get familiar with where your system is. The second is to keep an as-built diagram of it somewhere safe. One of the items on the Don't list is digging without knowing where the distribution pipes run.

This matters more in Muskoka than most places, because the townships now expect you to find it. The Township of Muskoka Lakes requires property owners to uncover the tank lids for its inspections. Lake of Bays asks owners of the systems it rates as higher risk to uncover both lids. If your lids sit under a foot of soil and a flower bed, that is a job for a dry afternoon in summer, not the week the inspector is due.

Risers help. A riser brings the access opening up to the surface so the tank can be opened without digging. SepticSmart asks that the lid be secured to the riser at all times. It also gives a warning that is worth repeating to anyone who will listen: never go into a septic tank. The gases and the lack of oxygen can kill.

Opening the tank every three to five years

The Ontario Building Code does not set a pumping calendar for septic tank maintenance. Article 8.9.3.4 says a septic tank must be cleaned whenever sludge and scum take up one-third of its working capacity. That is a measurement, not a date.

SepticSmart turns it into a routine: a licensed sewage hauler or onsite sewage system professional should take the cover off and inspect the system every three to five years, and pump out the solids and scum when they are due. The inspection is the part people skip. It is also the part that tells you whether you are pumping too often, which wastes money, or not often enough, which wastes a bed.

How a cottage changes septic tank maintenance

How fast sludge builds up depends on how many people use the system and how hard. A house with four people in it every day fills a tank at a steady rate. A cottage is different. It may sit empty for weeks and then host twelve people over a long weekend. Count the people-days, not the years. Muskoka Lakes puts it simply on its program page: work out a pumping schedule with your sewage hauler that suits your usage. A sensible hauler will measure the sludge and scum each visit and tell you when to book the next one.

When in the year to pump

If the tank is due, late summer is the better time at a seasonal cottage, not the day you close up. Sandy Bos, the onsite sewage system inspector for the Township of Muskoka Lakes, warned in Cottage Life that an empty tank can float if the water table is high in spring, and that even a concrete tank can float. Pumping in August gives it time to partly refill before freeze-up. Our cottage winterization post covers the rest of closing up.

The filter, once a year

An effluent filter sits at the tank's outlet and stops the floating scum and suspended solids from leaving with the liquid. It is cheap and simple, and it is the single best protection a leaching bed has. SepticSmart asks that it be checked and cleaned every year. If your system does not have one, the guide suggests adding one.

A blocked filter usually announces itself. When effluent starts coming up around the tank lids, the guide says the likely causes are a blocked effluent filter or a saturated bed. The filter is the cheap one to check first. You can have your hauler or installer clean it, which is the sensible choice if you are not comfortable working over an open tank. The rule about never entering the tank applies here too.

One more filter belongs in the house. SepticSmart suggests a lint filter on the washing machine's discharge pipe, so the lint is caught in the house rather than in the tank.

What goes down the drains, and how much

The bed was sized for a design flow, a set number of litres a day worked out from the number of bedrooms. It cannot tell where extra water comes from. SepticSmart's advice is about volume as much as content:

  • Spread the laundry out. Several loads on one Saturday is a flood for a bed. A load a day is not.
  • Fix leaks. A running toilet at a cottage can send a surprising amount of water to the bed between visits.
  • Keep the hot tub, the sump pump and the eavestroughs off the system. None of them should drain into it.
  • Water softener backwash and a garburator only if the system was designed for them.

Then the content. Cooking oil and food waste, hazardous chemicals, pharmaceuticals, cigarette butts and sanitary products all stay out. On septic additives sold to boost a tank, the guide is blunt: you don't need them.

At a cottage, the hard part is that the people using the drains on a busy weekend are often guests who have never lived with a septic system. A short note by the sink and in the bathroom does more septic tank maintenance than any product on a hardware store shelf.

Water and weight on the bed

The leaching bed works only if air and water can move through its soil. Anything that compacts the soil or drowns it shortens its life. The Building Code puts the duty in one sentence, 8.9.3.2.(2): the land around a sewage system has to be kept in a condition that will not damage it or impair how it works.

In practice, that means four things:

  • No vehicles. Do not drive or park over the tank or the bed. That includes the delivery truck, the boat trailer, and the neighbour who offers to bring his tractor over.
  • No paving. A driveway or patio over a bed seals it.
  • No trees or shrubs too close. Roots go looking for water and find the pipes.
  • Water directed away. Downspouts, driveway runoff and surface water should run away from the bed, not onto it.

The last one matters on Shield lots, where a raised bed can sit on a slope with bare rock above it. When a summer storm hits, that rock sheds water fast. If the grading sends it across the bed, the soil can be saturated before the house has used a drop. Drainage done right the first time is critical in our freeze-thaw climate, which is why it is part of our site preparation work alongside the well and septic.

Winter in Muskoka

Wastewater arrives in the tank warm, and the soil over a buried system does the rest of the insulating. What helps it most through a Muskoka winter is snow, left alone.

North Dakota State University Extension, writing for a climate at least as cold as ours, puts it plainly: fresh snow is an excellent insulator, and compacted snow is not. Its advice is not to drive any vehicle over any part of the system in winter. That includes ATVs, snowmobiles and cars, because packed snow insulates far less than undisturbed snow. It also says not to plow the snow cover off the system, and suggests a snow fence to keep traffic away.

Septic tank maintenance before the first snow

  • Mark the tank lids and the edges of the bed with tall stakes. That way whoever plows your laneway knows where to stop.
  • Keep the snowmobile trail and the skating path off the bed. The kids' toboggan run belongs somewhere else too.
  • Leave the snow where it falls. Don't shovel the lids clear to check on them.
  • If a system does freeze, call an onsite sewage professional. Don't pour anything into the tank to thaw it. Have them find out why it froze, so the fix lasts past one winter.

A cottage used every winter weekend has a different set of decisions from one that closes in October. If you are planning a place to use all year, the septic system is designed around that from the start. Our custom cottage building includes four-season builds, a cottage you never have to close.

When the township inspects

Muskoka owners are increasingly not the only ones checking. Several townships around the lakes now run their own septic inspection programs, and the rules differ from one to the next.

Muskoka Lakes has sorted more than 10,000 sewage systems by risk and inspects them zone by zone on a rotating cycle. A system's type, age and inspection history decide how often it is checked and what kind of inspection it gets. Owners must uncover the tank lids. Pumping is not required before an inspection. If you do pump, the township wants it done at least six weeks before, so the inspector sees the system working normally.

Lake of Bays began its program in 2026 with waterfront and near-water properties, checking each system every 10 years. Its instruction runs the other way: do not pump the tank before the inspection.

So read your township's notice before you book any septic tank maintenance around it. Doing the right thing at the wrong time can mean a second visit.

If your system has a treatment unit

Some lots, especially close to the water or on thin soil over rock, need a treatment unit that cleans the effluent further before it reaches a smaller bed. Those systems carry legal obligations as well as good habits. Under the Building Code, 8.9.2.3.(2), a treatment unit other than a septic tank cannot be operated without a servicing and maintenance agreement with someone the manufacturer has authorized. SepticSmart adds that these systems need effluent sampling. Muskoka Lakes asks treatment unit owners for their most recent service report or maintenance contract before it inspects. Keep that paperwork with everything else.

Keep a septic tank maintenance log

SepticSmart asks for one thing that costs nothing: keep accurate records. The as-built diagram, the permit, every pumping and filter cleaning, every service call and repair, and for a treatment unit the maintenance agreement and sampling results. A single folder or a page in a notebook will do.

The log earns its keep three times. It tells the hauler what was found last time. It answers the township's questions without a scramble. And it is the first thing a buyer's inspector will ask for if you ever sell.

The same guide asks owners on a private well to test the water for bacteria at least three times a year, in spring, summer and fall. Record those results in the same place. A change in the well water is one of the earliest signs that something nearby is not treating wastewater the way it should.

Where to start with septic tank maintenance

If you already own a system, start with the first item: find the lids, and find the paperwork. Then book an inspection if you cannot say when the tank was last opened. Good septic tank maintenance is mostly a matter of writing the next date down.

If you are building, start earlier. Septic design and installation are part of our site preparation, and septic permits and inspections are coordinated with the build. Where the bed goes, how water moves around it, and whether a plow or a driveway will ever cross it are all decided on the site plan. Ask for the as-built diagram from the permit file when the system is finished. It is the first page of your log. Our posts on septic system design and the cost to put in a septic system cover that stage. When you are ready, request a quote and we will reply within two business days.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should a septic tank be pumped in Ontario?

Whenever sludge and scum fill one-third of the tank's working capacity, which is the rule in the Ontario Building Code. The province's SepticSmart guide recommends having a licensed hauler or onsite sewage professional open and inspect the tank every three to five years to find out whether it is due.

How often should a septic filter be cleaned?

Once a year. SepticSmart asks that the effluent filter at the tank's outlet be checked and cleaned annually, and suggests adding one if your system does not have one.

Should I pump my septic tank before the township inspection?

It depends on the township, so read the notice first. Muskoka Lakes does not require pumping before its inspection, but if you do pump it wants that done at least six weeks ahead. Lake of Bays asks owners not to pump before its inspection.

Is it safe to drive a snowmobile over a septic bed?

No. North Dakota State University Extension advises keeping all vehicles, including snowmobiles and ATVs, off every part of a septic system in winter, because packed snow insulates far less than undisturbed snow and the system is more likely to freeze.

Do septic tank additives help with maintenance?

No. SepticSmart lists additives claimed to boost a tank's performance among the things not to use and says plainly that you don't need them. Regular septic tank maintenance does the job: an inspection every few years, pumping when it is due, and a clean filter.
