What Actually Drives the Cost of a Custom Build

Price per square foot is the first number everyone asks for and the least useful one we can give. These are the decisions that actually move the total on a custom home or cottage — and most of them are made long before anyone breaks ground.

Cottage-country lots have character: trees, rock, slopes, water. That character is why you bought the property — and it is exactly why preparing it takes real experience.

Site preparation is the stage nobody photographs for a brochure and almost everybody underestimates when they budget. It is also the stage that decides whether the rest of the build runs smoothly or spends the summer working around a problem that should have been solved in week one.

We’ll cover:

  • Why price per square foot tells you almost nothing
  • The lot decides more than the house does
  • Shape costs more than size

Why price per square foot tells you almost nothing

It is the first number everyone asks for and the least useful one we can give.

A square foot of hallway and a square foot of kitchen cost wildly different amounts to build. So does a square foot under a simple gable versus one under a vaulted ceiling with a dormer. When someone quotes a per-foot figure, it is an average of a specific house on a specific lot with specific finishes — and the moment any of those change, the average stops describing your project.

The number is also easy to make look good. Leave the garage out, or the basement, or the site work, and the figure drops without anything about the house changing. That is why two builders can quote what sounds like very different rates and be proposing nearly identical buildings.

What we would rather do is talk about the decisions that actually move the total, because those are the ones you control.

The lot decides more than the house does

On a cottage-country build the land often has more influence on the budget than the floor plan.

A flat, cleared, serviced lot with a municipal road at the edge of it is the cheap case. Everything that differs from that adds cost: distance from the road, slope, rock, trees, soil that will not drain, and whether water and waste are municipal or have to be drilled and installed.

None of that is visible in a plan price, and it is the single most common reason a budget that felt comfortable in January feels tight in May. Site preparation is where the lot shows you what it actually is.

This is also the part that rewards finding out early. A soils or septic assessment before the plan is finalised costs a fraction of discovering the same thing after the foundation is designed.

Shape costs more than size

A bigger simple house is frequently cheaper to build than a smaller complicated one.

Every corner is framing, sheathing, insulation, flashing and labour. Every roof plane that meets another roof plane is a valley, and valleys are detail work. Dormers, cantilevers, vaulted ceilings and changes in floor level all add structure and trade hours without adding much floor area.

That is not an argument for a plain box. It is an argument for spending complexity where you will actually see and feel it, and keeping it simple where you will not. A dramatic great room earns its cost; three roof changes over a mudroom usually do not.

The finishes are where budgets move fastest

The structural shell of a house has a fairly predictable cost. Two houses of the same size and shape, framed the same way, land close together.

Then the selections start. Cabinetry, counters, flooring, tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting, windows, doors, railings. Each has an enormous range, every choice is individually defensible, and the gap between a modest selection and a premium one across a whole house is large enough to buy a significant part of another trade.

This is why we would rather set a realistic allowance per category early and let you move money between them deliberately, than discover the gap at selection time. Trading a tile budget into a window budget is a decision. Finding out in month six that both were optimistic is not.

What is in the number, and what is waiting outside it

A great deal of budget pain is not about the price of anything. It is about scope that nobody put in the column.

Permits and approvals. Engineering. Septic and well. Driveway. Grading and final landscaping. Appliances. Window coverings. Decks. HST. Any one of these left out is a line that will arrive anyway, just later and without a plan behind it.

Our build packages exist partly to make this explicit: what is included, what is yours to handle, and where the line sits. A turnkey arrangement and a finish-it-yourself arrangement are not the same number, and they should not look like the same number.

If you are building a new home as your primary residence, the HST rebate may apply to part of what you pay. Eligibility and amounts are set by the Canada Revenue Agency and they change, so confirm the current rules for your situation rather than assuming last year’s apply.

Changes cost more the later they happen

Moving a wall on a drawing costs an eraser. Moving it after it is framed costs framing, electrical, insulation, drywall and schedule. Moving it after drywall costs all of that plus the finishes that were already on it.

This is the strongest argument we know for spending real time at the design and plan stage. The decisions are cheapest and the options are widest at exactly the moment the house feels least real.

It is also why we would rather have an uncomfortable conversation about the budget early than a comfortable one that turns into a change order in month four.

Where to start

Start with the lot and the scope, not the plan price. If you know what the land will demand and what is genuinely included, the plan becomes a choice rather than a gamble.

Bring us the lot and a rough idea of the house and we will tell you what we can see, what we cannot see yet, and what it would take to find out. That conversation costs nothing and it is the one that stops the budget surprising you later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you give me a price per square foot?

We can, but we would rather explain why it is unreliable first. The figure depends on the shape of the house, the finishes, the lot and what is included in the scope — change any of those and the average stops describing your project. A number built from your actual lot and plan is worth far more than a rate.

What usually blows a custom build budget?

In our experience it is rarely the price of materials. It is site conditions nobody assessed before the plan was fixed, scope that was never in the column — driveway, septic, landscaping, appliances — and finish selections made without an allowance to measure them against. All three are avoidable, and all three are avoided at the planning stage.

Is a turnkey build more expensive than finishing it myself?

The totals are not comparable without being specific about what each one includes. A finish-it-yourself package moves work and risk to you, which can suit someone with the time and the trades. It is not automatically cheaper once your own time and any subcontracting are counted. Our build packages page sets out where the line sits in each case.
