Permits & Approvals

We Speak Township

Zoning bylaws. Building permits. Conservation authority sign-offs. Entrance permits. Septic approvals. Rural and waterfront builds can involve a stack of approvals — and getting them in the wrong order can cost you a season.

After 50 years of building across Central Ontario’s townships, we know the processes, the people and the pitfalls. We prepare the applications, manage the back-and-forth, and keep your project moving while you get on with your life.

  • Zoning review before you commit to a design — or a property
  • Building permit drawings and applications, handled
  • Conservation authority and waterfront approvals
  • Septic permits and inspections coordinated with the build

What Building Permit Drawings Have to Show

A permit application is refused far more often for being incomplete than for being wrong. The drawings have to show the building, the lot and the relationship between them: site plan with setbacks, floor plans, elevations, sections, and the structural and mechanical detail the reviewer needs to confirm Ontario Building Code compliance.

Missing one of those does not get a conditional approval — it gets the file returned, and the clock starts again. Submitting a complete file first time is the single biggest thing anybody can do to shorten an approval.

Building Permit Drawings for a Garage, an Addition, or a Whole House

The same principle scales down. A garage and an addition need the same completeness as a house, just less of it. A detached structure over 10 square metres needs a permit under the Ontario Building Code, and anything with plumbing needs one at any size — see garages, ADUs and bunkies.

Zoning Comes Before the Building Code

Two different questions, asked by two different people. The Building Code asks whether the building is sound. The zoning by-law asks whether it is allowed to be there at all — setbacks, height, lot coverage, and whether a second dwelling is permitted. Zoning is where projects actually stall, and it is decided by the township rather than the province.

Building Permit Drawings and Township Approvals Across Central Ontario

Each township runs its own process and its own timelines. We have filed in most of them and keep dedicated pages for several: Bracebridge, Gravenhurst, Huntsville, Barrie, Orillia and Innisfil.

Who Else Has to Sign

The building department is rarely the only desk. On a rural or waterfront lot a file may also need the conservation authority, which permits work near water, shorelines and floodplains under Ontario’s Conservation Authorities Act, and a septic approval, which in much of Ontario is administered through the local health unit or the municipality.

An entrance permit may be required for a new driveway onto a municipal or county road. Each of these runs on its own timeline, and they do not queue neatly behind one another — which is why they are started in parallel rather than in sequence.

Why Applications for Building Permit Drawings Stall

Almost always because something is missing rather than because something is wrong. A missing site plan dimension, an unclear structural detail, a form filled in but unsigned. The review clock only runs on a complete file, so an incomplete submission does not buy an early place in the queue — it loses one.

The other common cause is a zoning conflict discovered late: a setback the design does not respect, or a lot coverage figure slightly over. Checking zoning before drawings are finalised rather than after is what avoids it.

Minor Variances and When They Are Needed

Where a design does not comply with a zoning by-law, a minor variance application to the local Committee of Adjustment is the route. It is a public process with notice to neighbours and a hearing, and it adds months rather than weeks.

Sometimes it is worth it and sometimes the design should move instead. That call is far easier to make at the drawing stage, which is another reason zoning is checked first.

Keep the Approved Set on Site

The stamped set is the version that was approved, and it is the version an inspector checks against. Building from a superseded print is a common and expensive mistake, because the inspection fails against the drawing rather than against the intent.

Inspections happen at defined stages — footings, backfill, framing, insulation and final among them — and each has to be called and passed before the next stage is covered up. Missing one can mean opening finished work to prove what is behind it.

Timing the Application Against the Season

Construction in Central Ontario is shaped by the ground being frozen for part of the year, so when a permit lands matters as much as whether it lands. An approval arriving in late autumn may mean a foundation poured in conditions that cost more, or a site sitting through winter.

Working backwards from the season somebody wants to break ground in, rather than forwards from when drawings happen to be ready, is the difference between a build that starts well and one that waits. That calculation is part of the planning work rather than an afterthought.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a permit submission need to include?

A site plan showing setbacks, floor plans, elevations and sections, plus the structural and mechanical detail the reviewer needs to confirm Ontario Building Code compliance. An incomplete set is returned rather than conditionally approved, which is the most common cause of delay.

Do I need a permit for a garage?

Under the Ontario Building Code a detached accessory structure needs a building permit once it exceeds 10 square metres, about 108 square feet. Most garages are well over that. Anything with plumbing needs a permit regardless of size.

How long does a building permit take?

It depends on the township and on how complete the submission is. The review clock only runs on a complete file, so the time spent getting the drawings right before filing is usually recovered several times over.

What is the difference between zoning and a building permit?

Zoning asks whether the building is allowed where you want it — setbacks, height, lot coverage, permitted uses. The building permit asks whether it is built to code. Zoning is decided by the township and is where most projects actually stall.

Do you file the application for me?

Yes. Preparing the drawings, filing with the township and handling the back-and-forth through review are all part of the service, including conservation authority sign-off where shoreline work is involved.

Do I need permit drawings for an addition?

Yes. A home addition needs a building permit and the drawings have to show how the new work ties into the existing structure, which is often the part a reviewer looks at hardest.
